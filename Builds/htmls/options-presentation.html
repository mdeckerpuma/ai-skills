Chapter I

Calls, Puts & the Contract

Every option gives one party a right and saddles the other with an obligation. The premium is the price of that right. Master this asymmetry first — everything else is built on top of it.

The Four Roles — Rights vs Obligations
RoleRight or ObligationYou Profit WhenMax ProfitMax Loss
Long Call RIGHT to buy 100 shares at the strike Stock rises above strike + premium paid Unlimited Premium paid — nothing more
Short Call OBLIGATION to sell 100 shares at strike Stock stays at or below strike Premium received — nothing more Unlimited
Long Put RIGHT to sell 100 shares at the strike Stock falls below strike minus premium Strike minus premium (stock to $0) Premium paid — nothing more
Short Put OBLIGATION to buy 100 shares at strike Stock stays at or above strike Premium received — nothing more Strike minus premium

Buyers have limited loss and unlimited (or large) upside. Sellers have limited gain and unlimited (or large) downside. The premium compensates sellers for taking on the obligation.

Moneyness

Where the strike sits relative to the stock

In the Money
ITM
Call ITM: strike below stock
Put ITM: strike above stock

Has intrinsic value. Delta near 1.00 (call) or −1.00 (put). Behaves like owning the stock. Most expensive — least extrinsic risk.
At the Money
ATM
Strike price equals stock price.

Zero intrinsic — all premium is extrinsic. Delta ≈ 0.50. Highest theta decay and highest vega sensitivity. Where sellers focus.
Out of the Money
OTM
Call OTM: strike above stock
Put OTM: strike below stock

No intrinsic value. Cheaper to buy. Delta near 0. Used for wings on condors and credit spreads.
Value Anatomy

Intrinsic vs Extrinsic

Every premium is two things. Only extrinsic decays — theta eats it daily. Only extrinsic collapses when IV falls. Intrinsic is permanent until exercise or expiration.

Call Intrinsic = max(0, Stock − Strike)
Put Intrinsic = max(0, Strike − Stock)
──────────────────────────────
Extrinsic = Total Premium − Intrinsic
// extrinsic is eaten by theta every day
// extrinsic collapses when IV falls
Example — SPY at $522
OptionPremiumIntrinsicExtrinsic
520 Call (ITM)$4.80$2.00$2.80
522 Call (ATM)$6.20$0.00$6.20
525 Call (OTM)$3.10$0.00$3.10
525 Put (ITM)$5.40$3.00$2.40

The ATM call has the most extrinsic — $6.20 of pure time value at risk.

Breakeven Formulas

Where you need the stock at expiration

Long Call BE = Strike + Premium Paid
// 520 call for $4.00 → BE = $524.00
Long Put BE = Strike − Premium Paid
// 520 put for $3.50 → BE = $516.50
Short Put BE = Strike − Premium Received
// Sold 515 put for $2.00 → BE = $513.00
Straddle BEs = Strike ± Total Premium
// Sold 520 straddle for $9 → $511 / $529
Chapter II

The Greeks — Measuring Risk

Greeks answer one question at a time: if only this one thing changes, how does my option's price move? In practice all of them shift simultaneously — understanding how they interact is the real edge.

Δ
Δ
Delta
Dollar change per $1 move in the stock
Delta is your directional exposure. A 0.45Δ call gains approximately $0.45 per $1 the stock rises (per share — multiply by 100 for a contract). Delta also approximates the probability the option finishes in the money.

Long positive Δ (calls) or negative Δ (puts)
Short opposite sign
SPY at 520. Own a 0.45Δ call.
SPY → 521: option gains ~$0.45 × 100 = $45
Deep ITM (Δ≈1.00) = dollar-for-dollar with stock
ATM (Δ≈0.50) = 50¢ per $1 move
Far OTM (Δ≈0.05) = barely moves
Γ
Γ
Gamma
How fast delta changes per $1 stock move
Gamma explains why delta does not stay still. Long options → positive gamma — each favorable move increases your delta, so you earn more on the next move. Short options → negative gamma — each adverse move increases how much delta works against you.

Gamma peaks ATM and explodes near expiration. Short ATM positions with under 7 DTE carry the most dangerous gamma exposure in options.
Sell straddle: Δ≈0, Γ≈−0.08
SPY falls $1 → delta becomes +0.08
SPY falls $1 more → delta becomes +0.16
Each move accelerates the loss. This is gamma risk.
Θ
Θ
Theta
Dollar gained or lost per calendar day
Theta is the daily erosion of extrinsic value. Only extrinsic decays — not intrinsic. Theta accelerates in the final 30 days non-linearly. This is why premium sellers target the 30–45 DTE window: fast decay, enough time to adjust if wrong.

Buyers pay theta — time costs you daily
Sellers earn theta — time works for you
ATM option at 45 DTE: Θ = −$8/day
Same option at 7 DTE: Θ = −$22/day
Decay nearly triples in the final week.
Sellers love 30–45 DTE. Buyers fear it.
V
ν
Vega
Dollar change per 1% move in implied volatility
Vega is the tension at the core of every options trade. Theta and Vega directly oppose each other. You cannot earn from time passing AND benefit from rising volatility at the same time. Choose a side — and choose based on IVR.

Buyers long vega — rising IV helps you
Sellers short vega — rising IV hurts you
Short straddle: Θ=+$14/day, ν=−$80 per 1% IV
10 days pass → theta earns +$140
IV spikes +6% → vega costs −$480
Net P&L: −$340 despite 10 days of theta

Chapter III

Volatility — The Pricing Engine

Volatility is the most important pricing input after the stock price itself. Two traders can look at the same option and reach completely different conclusions about its value — depending on where they think volatility goes from here.

Implied Volatility (IV)
What the market expects
IV is back-solved from current option prices — a forward-looking expectation, not a fact. High IV means the market fears large moves and options are expensive. Low IV means calm is expected and options are cheap.

High IV = expensive options = edge for sellers
Low IV = cheap options = edge for buyers
Daily Expected Move (1 SD) = S × IV% ÷ 16
// SPY 520, IV 16%: 520 × 0.16 ÷ 16 = $5.20/day
// 68% of days stay within ±$5.20
Historical Volatility (HV)
What the stock actually moved
HV measures realized price movement over the past 20–30 days. It is a backward-looking fact. The spread between IV and HV reveals structural mispricing.

IV > HV significantly = options overpriced for actual moves → sell
IV < HV significantly = options cheap for actual moves → buy
Edge Signal: IV vs HV spread
// IV=30%, HV=14% → overpriced → sell premium
// IV=14%, HV=28% → cheap → buy premium
IV Rank — IVR

Is IV high or low relative to itself?

A stock with 40% IV could be cheap (if it normally runs at 70%) or expensive (if it normally sits at 15%). IVR puts current IV in context of its own 52-week range.

IVR = (Current IV − 52wk Low) ÷ (52wk High − 52wk Low) × 100
0 – 30
Buy Premium
IV is historically cheap. Favorable risk/reward for buyers. Use debit spreads, long calls/puts, straddles. Rising IV will help you (positive vega).
30 – 60
Follow the Trend
No structural vol edge. Trade directionally. Condors work with caution. No strong vol signal — let price action lead.
60 – 100
Sell Premium
IV is elevated and likely to mean-revert. Credit spreads, condors, short straddles. Theta + falling IV = double benefit for sellers.
The Vol Crush Trap

When being right still loses

The costliest misunderstanding in options. You correctly predict the direction of a move — and still lose — because the move was smaller than the market feared, and the uncertainty premium evaporates when the event resolves.

The stock moved in the right direction by $7. You still lost $5.80. The $12 premium was priced for a potential $12+ swing plus the uncertainty premium of the event itself. Once earnings resolved, both the big-move fear and the uncertainty evaporated simultaneously. Vega destroyed more value than delta created. IVR = 94 before an event means sell the straddle — not buy it.
